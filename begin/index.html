Begin your journey

This is the one page that says what the rest of the site is for. No exercises, nothing to sign up to — just the handful of ideas everything else here rests on. Read it once and the rest will make more sense.

Start here · 12 min read

A question to carry: when something is beyond you right now, is that because of what you can do — or because of what you are trying to hold in your head all at once?

You are working inside real limits

You never have all the facts. You never have enough time. And you can only hold a few things in mind at once. So you do not find the best answer — you find one that is good enough, and you move on. That is not a flaw in you. It is what thinking with a real brain costs, and it is true of everyone who has ever been good at anything.

Nothing on this site removes those limits. What good tools do is give you more room inside them: they hold the details, so the part of you that decides what matters is free to do that instead. Most of that work is done by ordinary tools you already know — a list, a search, a spreadsheet, a reminder. What is new is that AI can reach those tools on your behalf, and explain them back in words you already use.

The trap: "more information means a better decision"

It doesn't, because how much you can hold is one of the limits. The same tool that surfaces three things worth knowing can bury them under thirty that aren't. More is only better up to the point where you can still see what you have — after that it quietly makes you worse, and it feels like being thorough the whole way down.

A reason, a sense of getting better, and a say

Three things keep a person going at something hard: a reason that matters to them, the feeling of getting better, and some say in how they do it. Take any one away and the same work starts to feel like a slog. That is the idea Daniel Pink set out in his book Drive, and it is worth knowing here because working with AI can move all three at once — and not always in the same direction.

The one that goes first is usually the feeling of getting better, because that feeling comes from doing the reps. If the tool takes every attempt, there is no moment where you notice yourself improving. This is also the part that stings if you are relearning work you were already good at: you were competent, and now you are not, and that is about lost reps rather than lost ability.

The reason is the piece no tool can hand you. A machine will happily lay out your options and show what each one costs. It cannot tell you which of them you actually care about — and if you ask it to, you will get something reasonable-sounding, and a ranking will have quietly become your purpose.

Trust is a forecast, not a feeling

Trust sounds like a feeling. It works better as a prediction: given what you have seen so far, you expect a person or a tool to behave a certain way — and you change the prediction when new evidence turns up. Faith asks you to commit without evidence. Trust asks you to commit on evidence that is never complete, and to keep paying attention afterwards. So the aim is not to reach the point where you can stop checking. It is to check the things worth checking.

There is a well-known way of picturing it, from The Trusted Advisor by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford. Trust goes up with three things — whether you have shown you know your subject, whether you do what you said you would, and whether people feel safe bringing you something awkward. It goes down with one: how much you seem to be in it for yourself.

trust = ( credibility + reliability + safety ) ÷ self-interest

Now the honest part, because it matters more than the formula. That is a way of thinking, not a measurement. None of the four parts arrives with a number, so nothing can really be added or divided. The parts are not even fixed — swap "people feel safe with you" for "people can predict how you decide" and it still works, which tells you they were a choice. And it can point the wrong way: sharing more of yourself is supposed to build trust, and sometimes it does the opposite. Keep it as a picture that helps you notice which part is missing. Do not keep it as a score.

With an AI, the trap is visible: a right answer and a wrong one look exactly the same, because both arrive fluent. Smoothness is a writing style, so it is evidence of nothing. Take the one claim that would cost you most if it were wrong — a date, a figure, a "the document says" — and ask for the exact words it relied on. Then go and read those words yourself.

Somewhere to put things down

Hold a phone number in your head while you dial it. That is working memory: a handful of things at once, gone in a blink. It is that small for everyone, and practice does not make it bigger. So the useful move is not to hold more — it is to put things somewhere you can reliably find them again. A note. A list. A page that keeps them for you.

Two things make that stick, and both feel worse than the thing most people do. Reading something twice makes the words familiar, and familiar is not the same as known — what actually works is putting the page away and pulling the idea back out before you check. And forgetting follows a curve, steeply at first, so if you meet something again just before you would have lost it, the gap until next time can be longer. A day, then a few days, then weeks. The memory pages do this for you rather than asking you to organise it.

Saving things feels like progress, and mostly isn't. A folder of clippings does nothing, because having something filed is not the same as being able to reach it. What changes that is linking: when one note names another, both become easier to find, and the collection gets more useful faster than it gets bigger.

You cannot be corrected on something you never made

A thought in your head has no edges. The moment you write it down, say it out loud, or try it on real work, it becomes something that can be wrong — and only then can you fix it. That is the whole reason this site keeps asking for something small back instead of just explaining at you. Reading about it does very little on its own.

Some people reading this have never used any of it. Others have thirty years of hard-won skill and have just been handed tools that make them feel like a beginner again. Both are ordinary, and neither is behind. What used to decide how far anyone got was whether somebody had the time to explain things at their pace, in a shape that made sense to them — and almost nobody ever got that.

What you let in

Most of what reaches you is not worth your attention, and deciding that is a skill rather than rudeness. You already have settings for it: what you read, who you listen to, which ideas you wave off before you have really looked. Almost nobody chose those settings on purpose — and they are the part of all this you can most easily change.

There are three ways to meet something new, not two. You can shut the door: reject it unless it is already approved. You can take everything in without checking, which is not open-mindedness, just gullibility. Or you can hold off on the no — let it in to be tested, and see whether it survives. Only that third one can correct itself, because the other two never find out they were wrong.

Settings can also freeze, and it usually happens when something starts counting for you. A score, a streak, a number that stands in for what you actually wanted. The number is easier to chase, so it quietly becomes the goal and the thing it was measuring drops away. Once that has happened you cannot fix it from inside the game, because the rules that broke it are the rules you would be using.

And nobody has ever been argued into an open mind. Being told to be more open does not do it, and neither does a good case well made — you can lose an argument and keep the door shut. What moves it is trying something and finding out. Which is why we would rather you tested any of this than believed it.

Agency, and what it is for

Two words get used as if they mean the same thing. Autonomy is nobody stopping you. Agency is being able to decide what you are aiming at, and then actually going after it. They come apart under pressure: someone left completely alone, with no aim and no wish to form one, has plenty of the first and almost none of the second. Nearly everything here is about the second.

Here is a rough way to picture how much you get done. On the top line, three things you bring: what only you know, what everyone around you already knows, and how you work. Then divide all of that by how much you keep out.

what you get done = ( what only you know + what everyone knows + how you work ) ÷ how much you keep out

Read it and the odd part stands out. Someone who knows a great deal and lets almost nothing in gets very little from it. Someone carrying less on the top line, who lets more in, often gets further. The top line also moves slowly — what you know and how you work took years, and you cannot add much to either this week. The bottom line can move today. Like the trust picture above, this is a sketch to think with and not a measurement; and the claim that you can genuinely keep less out than you do now is something we believe and are still testing.

So what is agency for? It gets spent. You have to want something and choose to go at it before any of the rest does anything at all — and the part of you that most changes your results only moves when you try things and see how they went.

The trade worth watching: AI clears the chores and answers before you have finished asking, so fewer things demand your time and you feel freer. But if it is also choosing what you read, what you write and what to want next, you are steering less. Freer and steering less is the exact shape of the problem, and it never announces itself. Hand over the volume; keep the decisions that carry what you care about.

The gap between two people

Everything inside your head is detailed. What you can get out of it — a sentence, a sketch, a file — is thin by comparison. Nobody ever receives your understanding. They receive what you managed to put outside yourself, and then they build something like it in their own head. That gap is not a fault in you or in them. It is the normal condition of two separate people.

Accepting that is harder than it sounds, and refusing it has two familiar shapes. One is frustration — why don't they just get it. The other is control: repeating yourself louder, managing every step, trying to win the argument. Both spend energy on a mind you cannot reach. Accept the gap and that energy goes somewhere it works: into the thing you are handing over, until it is good enough to be rebuilt at the other end.

This is also why three words are enough with an old colleague and a whole paragraph is needed with a stranger. Whatever you both already know can be left out. Building that shared ground on purpose — one agreed note, the same words for the same things, a template you both use — makes everything after it shorter. A new person needs more from you not because they are slow, but because there is less that can go unsaid.

Which makes working well with other people a practical skill rather than a personality you either have or you don't. It comes down to what you put outside your own head where someone else can get at it. It also looks, to us, like where most of the historical gain has come from: writing, shared records, peer review, keeping track of versions — every one of those improved what passes between people rather than anybody's brain. That is our reading of the history rather than something anyone has proved, and it is the bet this site is built on.

An AI has no idea what you meant. It has what you typed and whatever you gave it to read, and nothing else. That makes it a blunt, honest mirror of how clear your request actually was — and because the reply comes back in seconds, you find out in an afternoon what otherwise takes years to learn. Thin context in, thin work out. The habit you build fixing that is the same habit that makes you easier for people to work with.

Which is what this page is doing

The same words do not work for everybody. Some people need shorter sentences; some need denser ones. Some need different colours, or much stronger contrast, or their own software reading the page aloud. So you can change how this is said — the monkey face in the bottom-left corner sets a reading level and a colour scheme, and it holds for every page after it.

What it never changes is what is claimed. Simplifying something until it is no longer true would be the failure, not the goal. And this is the same job as explaining something to a colleague: you cannot get inside the other head, so all you can do is reshape what you hand over until it goes in easily. The tools turn out to be identical — plain words, one shared place to keep things, a note you can both point at — whether the other side is a person, a machine, or you in six months.

Try it on your own work

Pick one thing you have recently failed to get across to somebody — an idea at work, a request, something you explained twice. Use it to test the claim above rather than agreeing with it.

  1. Write down what they would have to already know. Everything your explanation quietly assumed: names, history, why it matters, what has already been tried. That list is what was missing, not their attention.
  2. Make one thing they can keep. Not a longer explanation — a note, a sketch, a filled-in example, three sentences with the assumptions stated. Something that survives without you standing next to it.
  3. Hand it over and watch what comes back. They will rebuild it slightly differently. That is the mechanism working, not a failure. The differences tell you exactly which part you had not actually said.

If you would rather practise where the feedback is instant, do the same thing with an AI: give it too little on purpose, look at what you get, then add what was missing and watch the answer change. The gap you are closing is the same gap.
