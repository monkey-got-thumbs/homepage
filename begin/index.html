Begin your journey

Welcome. This is the short version of what the site is for — the handful of ideas the rest of it builds on. Nothing to sign up to, nothing you need to know first. Five minutes, and then you can go and have a play.

Start here · 5 min read

You already know there's more

You wouldn't be here otherwise. There's something you'd like to do, or do better, or stop doing by hand — and somewhere in all the tools and all the talk, there's probably a way. That's the premise of this whole place. Not that you're behind. That there's more within reach than there used to be.

Good tools give you room

Nobody keeps it all in their head. Everyone works on a few things at a time, forgets the rest, and gets on with it — including the people who look like they have it all together. What good tools do is hold the details, so the part of you that decides what matters is free to do that instead.

Most of that is done by perfectly ordinary things: a list, a search, a spreadsheet, a reminder. What's new is that AI can go and use them on your behalf, and explain them back in words you already use.

What keeps you going

Three things, and they're worth knowing because they're easy to lose by accident: a reason that matters to you, the feeling of getting better, and some say in how you do it. Daniel Pink wrote a book called Drive about exactly this.

The one to watch is the middle one. If a tool makes every attempt for you, you never get the feeling of improving — and that's the bit that keeps you coming back. So let it take the boring volume, and keep hold of whatever you'd actually like to get good at.

Deciding what's worth your attention

You can't look at everything, so you're choosing all the time, usually without noticing. That's a skill rather than rudeness, and it's the easiest thing here to get better at. The move is simply to hold off on the "no" — let something in to be tried before you decide about it.

Shutting the door keeps out exactly the things that would have changed your mind. Leaving it wide open lets in anything at all. Trying things sits between the two, and it's the only one of the three that can put itself right.

Somewhere to put things down

Everyone's working memory is small, and practice doesn't make it bigger. So the trick isn't holding more — it's putting things where you can find them again. A note, a list, a page that remembers on your behalf.

Two things make an idea stick, and both feel like more effort than they are: reaching for it before you look it up, and meeting it again just before you'd have lost it. The memory pages do that part for you, so you don't have to keep track.

You get good by making things

An idea in your head has no edges. Write it down, say it out loud, or try it on something real, and suddenly it can be wrong — which is the only way it gets better. That's why there's a bit of doing on most pages here. Not a test. Just enough that something comes back at you.

Knowing when to trust it

Trust works better as a prediction than a feeling: from what you've seen so far, you expect a person or a tool to behave a certain way, and you change your mind when they don't. There's a well-known way of picturing it, from The Trusted Advisor by David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford. It goes up with whether you've shown you know your subject, whether you do what you said you would, and whether people feel safe bringing you something awkward — and down with how much you seem to be in it for yourself.

trust = ( credibility + reliability + safety ) ÷ self-interest

It's a way of thinking rather than a measurement — none of those parts comes with a number, and it can point the wrong way. Keep it for noticing which part is missing. With an AI the habit is smaller and more useful: a right answer and a wrong one both arrive sounding equally smooth, so pick the one claim that would cost you most if it were wrong, and check that one.

Steering your own work

Being left alone isn't the same as being in charge of what you're aiming at. AI is very good at handing you the first — it clears the chores and answers before you've finished asking — and it can quietly take the second, because the effort it saved you was the deciding. So hand over the volume, and keep the calls that carry what you actually care about.

There's a rough picture for this one too:

what you get done = ( what only you know + what everyone knows + how you work ) ÷ how much you keep out

The interesting part is the bottom. What you know took years and won't move much this week, but how much you let in could change today. Same caveat as before: a sketch to think with, not a measurement, and we're still testing it ourselves.

Getting through to other people

Everything in your head is detailed. What you can get out of it — a sentence, a sketch, a file — is thin by comparison. So nobody ever quite receives your understanding: they get what you managed to put down, and build their own version of it. That isn't a failure on either side. It's just what two separate people are.

Which is oddly freeing. Once you stop trying to close that gap by repeating yourself, you start doing the thing that works — making what you hand over good enough to be rebuilt at the other end. It's also why three words are enough with an old colleague and a whole paragraph is needed with someone new.

An AI is the same, only blunter: it has what you typed and nothing else, and it answers in seconds. Which makes it a very fast place to get better at being clear.

What this site does about it

The same words don't work for everybody, so you can change how things are said here. The monkey face in the bottom-left corner sets a reading level and the colours, and it holds for every page after it. What it never changes is what's being claimed — simplifying something until it isn't true would be the failure, not the goal.

That's the lot. Nothing else here assumes you've read it, so go wherever looks interesting — or start with the five short reads that build up from nothing at all.

These ideas aren't ours. The limits we all work inside come from Herbert Simon; the reason, the getting better and the say from Daniel Pink; the way of picturing trust from David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford; and the idea that tools should raise what a person can do, rather than replace them, from Douglas Engelbart.
