Begin your journey

You know there's more

That feeling is worth taking seriously, because it is the only fuel that actually works for this kind of work. The research is fairly blunt about it: for routine, mechanical tasks, a bigger reward does buy better performance — but for anything needing judgement or invention, it can make people measurably worse. Pay matters up to the point where money stops being the problem. After that, three things do the work: directing your own effort, getting better at something, and doing something that matters beyond you. "You know there's more" is just what wanting those feels like from the inside.

Which is also why the promise on the front page is a little further each day rather than something grander. Getting better is a direction, not a destination — the pull comes from moving, not from arriving. A day where you can do one thing you couldn't do last week is the whole mechanism. It is not modesty; it is the only version of this that keeps working.

The twelve-minute animation of the research is worth your time, and this is what it lands:

  • Bigger rewards reliably improve mechanical performance. On tasks needing even rudimentary thought, the same rewards made performance worse — a finding from MIT, repeated in rural India, where the highest-incentive group did worst of all.
  • Pay people inadequately and nothing else works. The purpose of paying fairly is to take money off the table so the work itself can do the motivating.
  • What is left over is those three: self-direction, getting better, and purpose. Not as sentiment — as the thing that actually predicts good work.
  • The evidence it isn't wishful thinking is sitting in plain sight. Skilled people built Linux, Apache and Wikipedia in their own time, for nothing, to a professional standard.
  • Firms act on it. Atlassian hands its engineers a day a quarter to work on whatever they like, and ships things that came out of it.

The reach itself, though, comes from other people — and collaboration is not a personality trait, it is a question of agency. Three things you bring, divided by the one thing that decides how much of them ever gets out:

agency = ( perspective + worldview + culture ) ÷ values

  • Perspective — your credibility: your maturity, and whether you show up competitive or cooperative.
  • Worldview — your ordering principle, your plan for interacting, your reliability.
  • Culture — intimacy, vibe, and the enormous stock of common knowledge you take for granted.
  • Values — the divisor, and the term you can actually move: your filter, your self-orientation, how you like to listen, how you speak.

Notice what is absent. Not one of those four is about mental effort, or how much you can hold in your head at once. They are about moving people and being moved — and like it or not, that is most jobs now, whether you teach, direct art or work in healthcare. The three qualities that make someone good at it land on the terms above almost exactly:

  • Attunement is perspective-taking — can you tune yourself to someone else and understand their view. That is perspective, under another name.
  • Buoyancy is staying afloat in what one salesman called an ocean of rejection. What predicts it isn't pep talk; it's how you explain failure to yourself afterwards — an ordering principle, so it belongs to worldview. Interestingly, questioning your own ability beforehand beats pumping yourself up.
  • Clarity is deciding what is signal and what is noise. Access to information stopped being an advantage a long time ago; curating it is the advantage. That is the filter, which sits in values.
  • And the note it ends on — looking for the ground where you and the other person are both better off — is culture: the shared stock that lets short messages work at all.

None of it is about capacity. All of it is about being, in the end, a bit more human.

Which is where trust comes in, and where it is usually pointed the wrong way. The familiar equation — credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, over self-orientation — gets used as a scorecard for other people. It isn't one. Put it next to the four above and it lines up term for term, and in the same positions: credibility sits inside perspective, reliability inside worldview, intimacy inside culture, and self-orientation inside values — which is the divisor in both. It is the same shape, read narrowly.

That correspondence is the whole point. The trust equation is an instrument for auditing your own agency, and nothing else. You run it inward, on your own three-over-one, until you have a defensible read on whether your own judgement can be trusted — and you keep working on the divisor, because it is the term that moves.

And that is why the three of them together are worth more than any one of them alone. Motivation gives you the reason to reach. Trusting your own agency is what makes you willing to hand something over without needing to control what happens to it. And the moment you can do that, someone else's agency stops being an obstacle to manage and becomes the thing that carries your idea further than you could carry it yourself. That is the "more" you can already feel. It was never going to come from working harder on your own.

The motivation research on this page is Daniel Pink's — Drive: The Surprising Truth About What Motivates Us (2009), as presented in his RSA lecture. Attunement, buoyancy and clarity are his too, from To Sell Is Human (2012) and the RSA short drawn from it — where the point about explanatory style predicting resilience is credited to Martin Seligman at the University of Pennsylvania. The way of picturing trust is David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford's, from The Trusted Advisor.
